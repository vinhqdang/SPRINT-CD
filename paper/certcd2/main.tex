\title[Certificate-Only Causal Discovery]{Certificate-Only Causal Discovery:
Monitoring-Valid Edges and Predictive-Fit Orientation}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Quang-Vinh} \sur{Dang}}\email{vinh.dq4@buv.edu.vn}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{British University Vietnam}, \orgaddress{\city{Hung Yen},
\country{Vietnam}}}

\abstract{Constraint-based causal discovery deletes an edge when a
conditional-independence test fails to reject, so a lack of power is reported
as a conclusion, and its output cannot be monitored as data arrive: an analyst
who reruns it at each sample size and keeps any edge ever reported accumulates
false edges. We study certificate-only discovery, which asserts a feature of
the graph only by rejecting a null that is true whenever the feature is absent.
The adjacency certificate is the minimum over conditioning sets of sequential
tests that a partial regression coefficient is zero. It bounds the probability
that a false edge is ever certified, uniformly over time and without
faithfulness; the bound is exact under Gaussian errors and asymptotic
otherwise. The orientation certificate compares sequentially fitted
forecasters for the two factorisations of a pair and certifies a direction only
when two model classes agree, so what it certifies is better predictive fit,
which identifies the causal direction in additive-noise models. In 150 random
graphs monitored from $n = 100$ to $6400$, CERT-CD reported a false edge in
$2.7\%$ of graphs under Laplace noise, against $27\%$ to $91\%$ for PC, GES and
DirectLiNGAM, at the price of leaving most non-adjacent pairs undecided and
a low recall at small samples; the rate stays below $9\%$ up to $16$ nodes. On
the Tübingen cause-effect pairs, under a protocol fixed before the run, the
orientation certificate committed on $12$ of $80$ scorable pairs and was right
on $11$; on nine held-out simulated families it was wrong on $5$ of its $83$
commitments, all in one family. Both certificates fail outside their models,
and we report where.}

\keywords{causal discovery, anytime-valid inference, e-values, sequential
testing, additive noise models, family-wise error control}
